## 1. Conversation

1. Reader: the person in the current exchange, with the context already shared there.
2. Purpose: answer a question, clarify a distinction, correct an answer, or make an authorized commitment.
3. Information: the direct answer and the reason needed to understand it. Include a next action only when the request calls for one.
4. Tone: the user's requested conversational style and the established relationship. Concision does not require formal prose or omitted particles that change the meaning.
5. Common errors: answering an adjacent question, unnecessary preamble, abrupt personal evaluation, and switching speech level without a reason.
6. Output: a ready-to-send reply. For an actual casual commitment, a short sentence such as `다시 확인할게.` can be sufficient.

### Explain an opinion so the reader can follow the choice

Before drafting a discussion reply, distinguish each participant's actual proposal, the known facts, and the sender's position on each point. A sender can adopt different parts of different proposals. Attribute the adopted point where it appears rather than opening with blanket agreement that the rest of the message contradicts. Keep the sender's new condition or suggestion separate from what a teammate originally proposed. Do not turn an opinion into a team decision or a promise to implement it.

Use the [evidence, reason, and claim check](korean-language-principles.md#connect-evidence-reasons-and-claims) for the relationship itself. Describing a structure or repeating a label is not yet an explanation of its benefit. State what the relevant fact changes for the person doing the work and why that matters under the supplied conditions. Distinguish a requirement from a choice, an expected effect from an observed result, and a consequence of a chosen option from something every option requires. Name a material tradeoff when the source establishes it; do not invent a benefit or disadvantage to complete a persuasive pattern.

Follow the requested information order. When the user asks for reasons before recommendations, let each point develop from the relevant fact through its implication to the proposed choice, with attribution where needed. Do not lead with the same recommendation and merely attach an explanation afterward. Keep the subject clear early so the reader knows what the reason concerns. This is not a rule to put every Korean conclusion last: preserve an explicitly requested answer-first summary or another already suitable structure. Avoid mechanical sentence counts and repeated paragraph formulas.

If the user asks why a previous recommendation is good or necessary, reassess its premise before defending it. A plausible rationale is not evidence. Explain what the available facts actually support, narrow an overstatement, and acknowledge when another option also satisfies the requirements. When essential facts are missing, identify the specific gap rather than inventing a cause or asking again for supplied information. Put reasoning needed by the recipient inside the sendable message; an explanation outside the draft cannot make that draft self-contained.

### Match the sender's voice through revisions

Use the user's latest draft and corrections as evidence of the intended voice, not as a collection of words to mechanically copy or prohibit. Preserve their accepted reasoning, attribution, and useful phrasing. Clarify an ambiguous object or missing connection within the authorized edit scope instead of rewriting the whole message to an assistant-preferred template. Follow the [editing contract](editing-and-review.md) for protected spans and local edits.

A less formal team reply may still use polite Korean. Review how the sentences develop and connect, not only whether they end in `해요` instead of `합니다`. Repeated agreement announcements, stock praise, unnecessary hedging, and a decorative `다만` can make a reply sound formulaic. Keep a real agreement or contrast when it serves the message. Rejecting a phrase in one draft does not ban that phrase everywhere; a user's later deliberate use or explicit approval is current evidence for that scope. Do not infer that a sentence was translated or that a nationality has one way of reasoning.

For a requested sentence-by-sentence explanation, keep each source sentence paired with its explanation in the original order. Distinguish what the speaker said, facts supplied separately, and the assistant's assessment. Define a necessary term where it is used, and explain the practical implication rather than substituting another technical term. Do not silently insert the assistant's proposal into the speaker's meaning. Use a list or table when the requested one-to-one correspondence benefits from it; a ready-to-send team message need not inherit that format.

### Fictional discussion example

The following people and event are fictional. Daon proposed retaining a venue map so first-time visitors can find the location. Yuchan proposed combining morning and afternoon schedules because participants currently have to check two sheets. Both want contact details to remain visible. The sender adopts those two points and adds a new proposal to check the printed copy. For a reason-first, conversationally polite reply, the message can say:

> 지금은 오전과 오후 일정을 보려면 종이를 따로 확인해야 하니까, 연락처는 그대로 두고 일정표는 유찬님 의견대로 한 장으로 합치면 좋을 것 같아요. 처음 오는 분들이 장소를 찾는 데 필요하니 약도는 다온님 말씀대로 남기고요. 여기에 인쇄한 뒤 글자가 잘 보이는지도 한 번 확인하면 어떨까요?

The example illustrates a supported reason, point-specific attribution, and a distinct additional suggestion. It is not a fixed wording or a source of facts for another discussion.
