## 8. Proposal or service introduction

1. Reader: someone deciding whether the proposed service addresses a real need or how it can help with their task.
2. Purpose: explain the confirmed difficulty, the proposed response, and any expected benefit the requested artifact needs to convey.
3. Information: start from what the audience is trying to accomplish and the user's account of what makes that difficult. Do not infer the problem backward from a feature or substitute an implementation difficulty for the audience's problem. When necessity matters, connect the difficulty to the decision or action it obstructs and explain what information or capability would help. Do not assume a goal or choice is already settled when finding it is the stated difficulty.
4. Tone: concrete, reader-facing prose. If an expected benefit is required, connect the feature and enabled action to the burden it could reduce or the opportunity, preparation, or decision it could improve. Merely reading information or making a plan is an action, not yet the benefit. Ground expectations in the confirmed design, distinguish them from observed results, and do not invent measurements, guaranteed success, or social effects.
5. Common errors: listing audience, problem, and features without explaining their relationship; hiding the actual function behind vague assistance; and repeating the author's expectations instead of explaining the projected change. State the supported action and object, but preserve uncertainty and genuine assistance when those are the meaning.
6. Output: connected prose that answers why the proposal is needed and what it offers. Check the actual saved passage, not just a checklist or reviewer explanation. Keep the reasoning framework private; do not impose fixed headings, a table, or an expected-benefits paragraph on every kind of document. If the user corrects the problem definition, return to the original account rather than repeating the narrowed interpretation in new words.

### Check a proposed improvement against current behavior

When asked which problem deserves priority, establish who is trying to do what, the supported obstacle or interpretation risk, its consequence for that task, and the criterion for prioritizing it. Put that relationship in the actual passage, not only a review note. A feature list or an attractive remedy does not establish need.

Separate current behavior, an observed difficulty, a possible consequence, and a proposed remedy. Existing functionality or a deliberate analysis limit is not automatically a defect. Do not call a plausible misunderstanding a measured user problem or the largest problem without comparative evidence. A recommendation may use an explicit criterion without inventing incident counts or survey results.

Check what the service already does before proposing additions. If guidance or a function exists, do not claim it is absent; identify the proposed change to wording, placement, scope, or use and explain how it addresses the stated problem. Preserve uncertainty about actual visibility or comprehension. If an observed defect is required but unsupported, obtain evidence or identify the limit rather than manufacture a problem. Include a proposed remedy and a relevant assessment method only when the question asks for them, and match that check to the claimed improvement. A requested investigation plan is a recommendation, not evidence that the author has already committed to it.

Rewriting a proposal does not authorize adding implementation, testing, survey, or follow-up commitments. Unknown research or measurement status does not establish that research never occurred, that findings were not used, or that the author rejected them as a basis. State the supported problem and proposal without inventing that negative contrast. Explain an evidence limit when requested or when omission would misleadingly imply an observed result; do not add an unrequested research-status report to otherwise supported copy.
