# Context and Audience Boundary

Apply this mandatory companion before drafting, revising, or reviewing Korean, including a short conversational reply. It is bundled with this skill; no separate skill or installation is required. Use the established context for a follow-up, and reconsider it when the speaker, audience, timing, setting, or purpose changes. Keep the analysis private unless the user asks for it.

## Establish the speaking situation

Recover these distinctions from the request, supplied material, and preceding exchange. They guide wording, not a questionnaire or a required output template.

1. **Speaker and represented voice:** Identify whose words the result will be. The person asking for a draft, its sender, the assistant, and a person mentioned in the text need not be the same. In a message written on someone's behalf, align first-person expressions and commitments with the intended sender. Do not turn another person's action into the speaker's action or invent authority to speak for a group.
2. **Addressee and relationship:** Identify who receives the words and the relationship actually established. Consider familiarity, roles, requested formality, and the destination's convention together. Do not infer age, gender, rank, intimacy, or a name from a role alone. Choose endings, pronouns, address forms, and request strength as a coherent whole; changing only the final ending is insufficient.
3. **Time and conversational stage:** Distinguish when an event happens, when the text is sent, and when it will be read. Preserve whether an action is planned, ongoing, or complete, and whether a message initiates contact, answers something, or follows up without a reply. Interpret `오늘`, `내일`, `이번`, and similar expressions against the supplied reference time, not automatically the machine's current date. For delayed or persistent text, use a supplied date when a relative expression would point to the wrong day. Do not invent a date, deadline, elapsed period, or urgency.
4. **Place, channel, and shared access:** Check whether people share a physical space, screen, object, or earlier message. A gesture or `여기` may identify a place in person but not over a phone call. Use a known location or object name when needed; do not invent one. Distinguish private messages, public notices, meetings, email, UI text, and spoken material. Public visibility does not itself make people strangers, and familiarity does not authorize publishing private information.
5. **Intended act and force:** Determine whether the text answers, asks, requests, suggests, declines, apologizes, reports, or makes a commitment. Preserve that act and its actual urgency. Do not turn a suggestion into an order, an availability question into a confirmed arrangement, or an intention into a completion report. Politeness must not weaken a required action or create a new promise. Drafting a request is not permission to perform it.
6. **Shared knowledge and reader need:** Use adjacent headings, preceding text, and available visuals to identify what the reader already knows. Omit an established subject or explanation only when the intended reader can still identify the actor and target. Name a changed speaker, actor, or referent when omission would transfer responsibility or make the words ambiguous. Do not repeat context merely because it was supplied to the writer.

Follow explicit user instructions within their scope. A chat's casual register does not set the voice of an attached public document, and a professional document's voice does not override the user's conversational style. Changing the audience may change wording and necessary explanation, but not the underlying facts, ownership, or authorized commitments.

## Decide what belongs in the result

Separate information the reader needs from the writer's working material. A verified fact is not automatically appropriate or authorized for publication.

1. **Need:** Keep facts, reasons, conditions, actions, risks, and evidence that support the intended reader's understanding or decision. Keep private strategy, drafting history, rejected approaches, and internal evaluation criteria out when they do not serve that purpose. Describe the result directly instead of contrasting it with an irrelevant discarded draft.
2. **Permission:** Respect the authorized audience and disclosure scope. Do not move personal details, internal identifiers, operating figures, or partial results from a private exchange into public copy merely because they appear in the source. If a required disclosure is unclear, ask about that detail before publishing it. An authorized internal report may need information a public notice does not.
3. **Duplication and precision:** Distinguish facts supplied to the writer from information the intended reader already knows. Preserve a supplied time standard, unit, scope, or comparison condition when it affects how a value is interpreted or what the reader should do. Omit such a condition only when it is already clear in the reader's explicitly shared context or adjacent visible content, such as a heading, date field, table, or preceding sentence. Retain exact dates, identifiers, and technical detail when they affect the reader's action, validity, deadline, reproducibility, or an explicitly requested audit. Technical documentation may need an exact identifier where general prose needs its meaning.
4. **Truthfulness:** Omission must not make a partial result look complete, a proposal look implemented, or a limited observation look general. Keep necessary qualifications and comparison conditions, including denominators when relevant. Preserve a needed fact rather than hiding it under a blanket rule against internal detail; ask or narrow the claim if truthful publication is otherwise impossible.

These are audience decisions, not bans on individual words. Comparisons, change histories, rationale, and private reflection belong when the requested artifact needs them. ## Handle missing context proportionately

Use what the source already establishes; do not ask the user to fill every category. When a missing relationship or referent would change the meaning, responsibility, disclosure, or commitment, ask a focused question. When it does not, use wording that avoids the unsupported assumption and still answers the request. Do not ask for a person's title merely to add an unnecessary salutation, or invent a speaker to make a sentence feel complete.

If the requested output must contain only a finished sentence but essential meaning cannot be resolved, do not fabricate it to satisfy the format. State the smallest needed clarification. Preserve exact quotations and confirmed wording unless the user authorizes editing them; distinguish those protected words from a draft explicitly submitted for revision.

## Check the actual wording

Read the result as the intended recipient at the stated time and place. Check whose action or promise the words express, what the recipient can identify, what response is requested, and what information is revealed. Verify that the intended relationship, timing, and act survive compression and polishing. For a scheduled event, check that the reader can identify its date using only the result and explicitly shared context; when neither establishes the year, retain the supplied year rather than assuming the writer's current year is shared. In a multi-audience request, check each version against its own situation without changing the shared facts. Deliver the requested text, not this analysis.
